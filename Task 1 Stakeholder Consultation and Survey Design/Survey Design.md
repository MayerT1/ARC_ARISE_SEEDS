# Background and context

The proposed multistate Greenbelt Corridor consists of 12-county, 8-subregion landscape across the Northern Alabama (Madison, Marshall, and Jackson counties) and South-Central Tennessee (Franklin, Coffee, Grundy, Marion, Sequatchie, Bledsoe, Van Buren, Warren, and DeKalb counties). Covering approximately 95,232 km² and home to 4.41 million residents, the region represents a diverse landscape of urban centers, agricultural valleys, and rural forest communities.

High-resolution Earth Observation (EO) assessment, drawing on National Agriculture Imagery Program (NAIP) aerial imagery and National Land Cover Database (NLCD) classifications, provide an important baseline for understanding this diversity. The assessment demonstrates different types of  land-use and socio-economic characteristics across the corridor. Madison County, for example, represents a rapidly urbanizing metropolitan center, with 23.2% of its land classified as developed. Warren, Coffee, and Franklin counties have substantial agricultural landscapes, with farmland accounting for approximately 24.5%–33.6% of their land area. Further east, the more rural plateau communities of Marion, Grundy, Sequatchie, and Bledsoe counties retain extensive forest cover, ranging from 65% to 79%. Several of these communities are also classified by the Appalachian Regional Commission (ARC) as transitional, at-risk, or distressed, highlighting the different economic and development challenges present across the corridor.

Although EO data provide a detailed picture of existing land use, forest cover, and ecological connectivity, they cannot fully explain how changes in the landscape may affect local economies and community well-being. Understanding the potential economic returns of the Greenbelt Corridor therefore requires going beyond physical land-cover measurements to examine how residents, landowners, businesses, and regional institutions experience and value these changes.

To address this gap, the proposed survey instrument combines structured stakeholder engagement with targeted economic valuation. The identified 307 regional stakeholders, include decision-makers, funders, implementation partners, gateway businesses, extension agents, and landowners. The survey is aimed at collecting primary socio-economic information on local property-value expectations, land-market transactions, visitor spending, regional supply-chain relationships, public-sector fiscal priorities, and willingness to pay (WTP) for anticipated corridor-related benefits. Together, these data will provide the empirical foundation for applying Spatial Hedonic Pricing, Contingent Valuation, and Multi-Regional Input-Output (MRIO) models. By linking observed landscape characteristics with stakeholder perspectives and economic behavior, the assessment will help establish a baseline against which the economic and social outcomes of Greenbelt Corridor implementation can be evaluated over time.
